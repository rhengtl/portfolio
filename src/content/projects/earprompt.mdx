EarPrompt is a small device worn during a conversation. When the other person finishes speaking, it
whispers one short coaching tip into the wearer’s earbud: a nudge on how to respond, never a script.
It was my team’s undergraduate thesis at the Polytechnic University of the Philippines,
*EarPrompt: A Real-Time Conversational AI Assistant for Enhancing Verbal Communication*, with four
named researchers. This page covers the software, most of which I wrote; the research, evaluation
and manuscript belong to the whole team.

## The problem

Speech anxiety and cognitive overload can make a person go blank in the middle of a conversation.
For someone working with a speech-language pathologist, that moment is exactly when help is most
useful and least available: practice apps give feedback after the fact, and phone assistants need
you to look away and ask.

We wanted a cue that arrives *during* the conversation, privately, in the ear. That set the
constraints:

- **It must not speak for the user.** The tip says *how* to respond, never the words to say — the
  prompt forbids writing a literal reply.
- **It has to know who is talking.** A tip triggered by the wearer’s own voice is noise.
- **It must be short and in the user’s language** — natural Taglish, one sentence.
- **It runs on a battery** in a small enclosure, so the device can’t host large models.

Around that loop, the device runs a small **Flask API** that the companion app uses to start and
stop listening, switch between automatic and push-button turn detection, register a voiceprint,
calibrate room noise, change the voice and tone, and read battery level from an INA219 sensor on
the power board. The wearer’s *own* speech isn’t wasted: it goes to a voice-analysis module that
measures pitch, intensity, jitter, shimmer and harmonics-to-noise ratio with Praat (via Parselmouth)
and compares them with the user’s baseline.

- **The audio and AI pipeline** — voice-activity detection, speaker verification, transcription,

  the current pipeline module.
- **The device API and hardware control** — the Flask endpoints the app calls, the button and LED

- **Voice analysis** — the acoustic measurements and the baseline comparison that raises alerts
  such as "pitch too high" or "unstable pitch".
- **Most of the Flutter app** — the therapist’s patient list and session history, voice
  registration, device Wi-Fi setup and the dashboard.

## Key technical challenges

### Telling the wearer apart from the other person

A tip should follow the *other* person’s turn. Each utterance is embedded with an ECAPA-TDNN model
and compared, by cosine similarity, with the centroid of the wearer’s enrollment embeddings
(outliers are filtered out at registration). Scripted enrollment and natural conversation produce
noticeably lower same-speaker scores than a model card would suggest: in real conversations the
wearer typically scored 0.40–0.65 against their centroid and conversation partners 0.05–0.25, so
the threshold was set at 0.35.

Short replies were the hard case. With a 0.8 s minimum, a quick "yes" or "okay" from the wearer
was too short to embed, got filed as the other person, and set off a tip at exactly the wrong
moment. I lowered the minimum to 0.4 s of speech after silence trimming, which covers those
replies.

**Trade-off:** anything still too short to embed reliably is treated as the wearer’s. The system
would rather stay quiet than whisper a tip at the wrong moment.

### Knowing when someone has finished speaking

Energy-based silence detection alone kept "hearing" speech whenever background noise kept the
level up, so turns didn’t end. I added Silero’s neural voice-activity detector on 32 ms windows,
with gain for soft or distant speech and a room-noise calibration step in the app. Energy is still
the fallback when the detector is unavailable or undecided.

### Latency with three cloud services in a row

Speech-to-text, the LLM and text-to-speech run one after another, so every hop counts. Tips are
capped at one sentence (12 words, 30 tokens), and instead of waiting for the full LLM reply, the
tokens are forwarded to the text-to-speech WebSocket as they arrive, so audio generation starts
before the LLM has finished.

Memory needed a bound too. A context-window overflow found in testing was fixed by updating the
prompt-truncation logic, and the current code sends a compact summary rather than the transcript:
at most ten keywords and two short snippets from the last five turns. The thesis’s cost analysis
(Fig. 7) shows
the effect of keeping more turns: tokens grow linearly rather than running away, and even 25 turns
of context cost under seven US cents per 30-minute session.

Two more measurements from the thesis:

- **Power and runtime (estimated, not measured on a discharge test):** 8.88 W average and 13.64 W
  peak from component ratings, giving about **3 hours** at average load on the two 18650 cells.
- **Maintainability:** five logged fixes took an average of **1.6 hours** to diagnose, patch and
  validate, the longest being the context-window overflow (2.5 h).
The acceptability survey used a 4-point scale. The overall mean was **2.81 ("High")**. Accuracy of
the tips scored highest (3.00 from both groups). Reliability scored lowest (2.64): the patient rated
it "Low" (2.40), citing glitches, unclear malfunction alerts and weaker performance in long
sessions.

## Limitations and what I’d do next

- **The evaluation is small and measured acceptability, not therapeutic effect.** One patient and
  three speech-language pathologists took part in a single post-use evaluation. The thesis itself
  says the results show technical viability, not clinical effectiveness.
- **3.8 seconds is slow for conversation.** It avoids the breakdown range, but tips can arrive
  after the moment has passed. The next step is an on-device or edge language model and cached
  voiceprints, which would also remove the dependency on internet access.
- **Speaker verification was tuned, not formally measured.** I’d record a labelled set of real
  sessions and report false-accept and false-reject rates instead of relying on observed score
  ranges.
- **Hardware:** a directional microphone array with noise reduction, and a smaller, cooler
  enclosure — the reliability complaints came from exactly these areas.
