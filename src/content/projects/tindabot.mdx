TindaBot helps a small-store owner plan the next supplier trip: what to buy, how many, and how much
cash to bring, with a plain-language reason behind every number. It works offline on a budget
phone, and I built it alone, from the specification to the device testing.

## The problem

A *sari-sari* store is a neighbourhood shop, usually run from a home. Before each trip to the
supplier, the owner has to answer four questions: *ano ang bibilhin, ilan, kailan, at magkano ang
dadalhin* — what to buy, how many, when, and how much money to bring. Today that's done from memory
and a notebook, so items run out before payday and cash runs short at the supplier.

The constraints shaped every decision:

- **Budget Android phones and patchy data.** It has to work fully offline, with no account.
- **The numbers must be explainable.** An owner won't trust "buy 3 cases" without knowing why,
  and an estimate built on little data has to say so.
- **Taglish by default**, with an English switch.
- **Nothing is lost.** Records are money and *utang* (customer credit), so corrupting them is the
  worst possible bug.

The design choice that everything else rests on: **the event log is the only source of truth**.
Stock levels, the shopping list, profit and every customer's balance are derived from it on every
change, never stored and patched. That makes the numbers reproducible and testable, and it means
sync only has to move immutable events.

## My contribution

TindaBot is a solo project. I wrote the specification (a blueprint that defines every rule the app
applies), the TypeScript domain model, the independent Python reference model, the Taglish and
English interface, the sync engine and database migrations, the AI proxy, and the validation on an
emulator and a physical phone.

## Key technical challenges

### Numbers an owner can trust

The app uses two kinds of estimate. When the owner has counted the shelf, it measures real usage
between counts, weighted toward recent weeks. When there are only purchases, it estimates how fast
the owner goes through stock from the purchase rhythm — and is explicit that this is *not* sales and
that it doesn't know what's left. Each estimate carries a confidence level, uncertain quantities are
shown as a range, and every line has a *bakit* ("why") sheet that explains it in plain words.

To make the arithmetic itself trustworthy, I wrote the rules twice: once in TypeScript for the app
and once, independently, in Python. The Python model generates the expected values, and the
TypeScript tests have to match them exactly.

### Staying fast on years of data, on a budget phone

Rebuilding everything from the full event log is simple and correct, but I had only ever measured it
on a small store. So I generated a fixture of 200 products, 40 customers and 19,585 events — about
two years of daily use — and profiled it on a realme C55. Opening the app took 3.3 seconds, and every
save froze the screen for up to 1.7 seconds.

The rule wasn't the problem; the way it read the log was. It re-scanned the whole log once per
product, and once per customer per week for the weekly summaries. Grouping the log by product and by
customer once per recompute made it 2.6× to 8.3× faster (Fig. 2), with identical output — a randomized test
holds the grouped path against the original one.

**Trade-off, decided deliberately:** opening the app still takes about 2 seconds at two years of
data, mostly reading the event log from storage. I chose not to add a cached snapshot or windowed
loading, because the full log as the single source of truth is what keeps the numbers trustworthy.

### An AI helper that can't corrupt the books

The AI is optional and boxed in. It **drafts** entries from a receipt photo or a typed or spoken
note, and nothing is saved until the owner reviews and confirms. When the owner asks a question, the
model calls tools that run on the phone against local data, and every number in its answer must
cite the field it came from. The app checks each figure against that source and marks anything it
can't verify as unverified. The Gemini key stays in a serverless function with per-account limits
of 30 requests a minute and 300 a day.

### Not losing data when something goes wrong

Testing failure modes on purpose turned up a serious bug: importing a backup in *replace* mode ran in
two steps, so an interruption between them deleted the store and left the import without its
history. I made the whole import a single transaction, so an interrupted import now leaves the
database exactly as it was. The same pass replaced an endless loading screen, when the browser blocks
storage, with an error that says what to check and a working *Try again*.

## Limitations and what I'd do next

- **The estimates are only as good as the entries.** Purchases alone give pattern-based estimates;
  counts make them accurate. The app says this in its own wording.
- **Data lives in the browser.** Clearing site data removes it, so the app nudges owners to export
  or turn on cloud backup.
- **iOS hasn't been tested**, and the free database plan pauses after about a week idle (the app
  keeps working and syncs again when it resumes).
- **Next:** put it in front of real store owners for a few weeks and measure whether the list
  matches what they actually buy, then revisit the 2-second start-up if real stores reach that size.
